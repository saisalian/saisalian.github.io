For a good while I looked after security for more than 70 Microsoft 365 tenants at a managed service provider. Now I work inside one organisation. I genuinely thought going from many to one would be a bit of a holiday. Spoiler: it wasn't. The tech was the easy bit. The hard bit was learning that you can't fix a house you live in the same way you fix one you're only visiting.

## Life as a visitor

MSP work is a bit like being the person who calls round to check everyone's locks. You knock, you look, you fix, you're out the door before the kettle has boiled, and on to the next one. And the next. And the next. Some are tiny, some are big, and each one has its own personality.

Do that long enough and you start seeing the same things in every house. A few problems showed up so often I could practically call them before I'd even logged in:

- MFA with gaps in it, usually around the accounts that mattered most. Of course.
- Stale accounts belonging to people who had left a long, long time ago.
- Shared mailboxes that still let someone sign in to them directly.
- Workarounds propping up legacy systems. They were meant to be temporary.........Years ago.
- Systems set up from a default rather than shaped around how the organisation actually works.
- Licences paid for and barely used. My favourite: tenants licensed for Conditional Access with not a single policy switched on. That's like buying a fancy alarm system and leaving it in the box. Still sealed.

If you work in-house, go on, spend half an hour checking your own tenant for these. I'd bet you find at least one.

That's the gift of breadth. See the same problems often enough and you build a mental checklist, and you get fast.

## Moving in

Working inside one organisation is different. You're not visiting any more; you live there. And when you live in a house, you find out why the back door is held shut with a chair. Someone put that chair there for a reason. Maybe not a good reason... but a reason.

My biggest surprise wasn't the systems. It was the process. At the MSP, the answer was often "apply the standard fix and move on". Inside one organisation, I own each process, and the fix has to fit the organisation, not just the template. Every change goes through the organisation's own way of doing things: people to talk to, timing to agree, and a change process that exists for good reasons (even on the days it really doesn't feel like it).

Here's the honest version. Early on, a fairly small hardening change took more than three weeks to go live. At the MSP? Done before lunch. Here, a change like that needs the right people to agree, the right time to apply it, and confidence that nothing else breaks when it does.

Anyone who has stood behind me in a queue knows patience isn't exactly my strongest skill, so that was a hard pill to swallow. But rushing doesn't work here, and pushing harder mostly just makes you tired. Ask me how I know. I've had to build up a patience and resilience I didn't know the job needed. Not everything changes when I want it to. Some things change later, and better, because they went through properly.

## What I kept, and what I dropped

**Kept: juggling.** MSP work trains you to look at several things at once. A surprising amount of security work is just... waiting. A script grinding through, a query taking its sweet time. So instead of staring at the progress bar, I pick up the next thread and come back to the first one when it's ready. Same skill as cooking dinner, really. Rice on, chop the onions, check the rice. Nobody stands there watching the rice. Well, nobody should.

**Dropped (still dropping, honestly): the urge to patch everything I see.** Now I try to stop and ask a few questions first. What are the options? Why this one over the others? What groundwork do I need before I propose it? A fix with good reasoning behind it lasts longer than a quick one. It's also way easier to defend when someone asks "why?", and inside an organisation, someone always asks why. Always.

## Why I'm writing this

Mostly for myself, if I'm honest. The internet is full of brilliant people doing brilliant things, and that's great. But I think it's useful to hear from normal people too, the ones working it out as they go. Life is too short to learn everything from your own mistakes, so you're welcome to some of mine.

## The takeaway

No experience is wasted. Everything counts. Breadth taught me patterns. Working inside one organisation is teaching me judgement: not just what's wrong, but what the right fix is for this place, at this time. I needed both, and I wouldn't swap either.

If you're making a similar move, the one thing I'd say is: go in with an open mind. If you walk in already sure how everything should work, you'll learn very little. Leave the door open (the front one, not the one with the chair) and you'll pick things up along the way.
